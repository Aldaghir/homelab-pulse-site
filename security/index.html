Security

Homelab Pulse can restart services and open a shell on your server. How to tell us about a problem in private, what guards each step, and what it does not guard against.

01 Reporting

Found a problem? Tell us in private

Please do not open a public issue about it, nor publish it, before a fix is out.

  • Email a reportandroid@aldaghir.com

The same, for tools: security.txt

Where
By email to android@aldaghir.com. Once the repository is public, also through GitHub’s private vulnerability reporting there.
What to send
The version affected (the app’s from Settings › About, the agent’s from hlpulse-agent version), what someone could do with the problem, and the steps to reproduce it.
In 72 hours
You hear back that your report arrived.
In 7 days
It has been assessed.
The fix
Made in private and released in both editions of the app together. Its details are published after that.
Credit
Your name goes with the fix, unless you would rather it did not.
In scope
The agent API’s authentication and authorization, pairing, certificate pinning, the check of Cloudflare Access, the secrets vault, the terminal, the push relay’s encryption, and what the app stores on the phone.

02 The model

What guards your server

From pairing to the release you install, each step and what it rests on.

  1. SHA-256

    Pairing you can trust

    The QR code carries the agent’s certificate fingerprint, and on your home network the app trusts that certificate alone. Away from home it goes over HTTPS through Cloudflare Access, with the phone’s own token as well. A pairing code works once, within ten minutes, and an address that keeps guessing is locked out.

  2. 256-bit · viewer · operator · admin

    A token and a role for each phone

    Each phone has its own token, which the agent keeps only as a hash, and a role that decides every request it may make. Revoking a phone ends its live data, its log streams and its shells at once.

  3. ECDSA P-256 · Keystore

    Your fingerprint, checked by the server

    A new terminal, stopping or restarting, cleanup, rebooting or powering off, alert rules, integrations, checks, invites, devices and remote access each need a fresh signature over that very request. It comes from a key in your phone’s Keystore that signs only within 30 seconds of your fingerprint or screen lock, and never leaves the phone. Without it the agent refuses: a copied token can read alerts, not act.

  4. Cloudflare Access

    No port open on your router

    Away from home, the app reaches the agent through a Cloudflare Tunnel behind Cloudflare Access. The agent checks Access’s signed token as well as the phone’s own, on a listener kept for the tunnel.

  5. X25519 · AES-GCM

    Push notifications only your phone can read

    Push notifications are encrypted on the server for each phone, with a key the two exchanged at pairing. The relay and Firebase carry them without seeing what they say. Alerts sent to Telegram are not sealed this way (see the limits).

  6. AES-256-GCM

    Keys that stay on the server

    Your Telegram, Cloudflare and Uptime Kuma keys are kept encrypted on the server, with a key only the agent’s user can read. The app can replace them but never read them back, and logs leave them out.

  7. no sudo · exposure 1.7

    An agent with the least it needs

    It runs as its own user, without sudo, inside systemd’s sandbox. A small helper does the few things that need root, only in the fixed forms its configuration allows, and Docker is reached through a filter that refuses creating containers and exec.

  8. Android Keystore · PBKDF2

    What your phone keeps

    Tokens and keys are encrypted with a key the Keystore never lets out. Nothing of the app’s goes to cloud backup or to a new phone’s transfer, and a backup file you make is encrypted with your password. The copy of what you last saw, kept for offline use, is not encrypted by the app (see the limits).

  9. Sigstore

    What you install is checked

    The install script installs the agent only when the release’s checksums carry a valid signature from the project’s release workflow and the download matches them. The code’s dependencies are checked for known vulnerabilities on every change and every week.

03 Limits

What it does not guard against

Choices made on purpose, so that you can plan around them.

  1. An admin phone can do what you can

    Through the terminal, an admin phone gets your shell on the server. Make admins only of phones you trust, and keep the app lock on.

  2. The root helper has no sandbox

    It runs as root, unconfined, so that the shells it opens can use sudo. It does nothing that its configuration, owned by root, does not allow.

  3. Telegram reads what it carries

    If you set up Telegram, alerts from the level you choose go to your own bot as ordinary Telegram messages, and so does a critical alert no one has acknowledged after a few minutes (5 by default). Telegram can read them. If that matters to you, keep Telegram off, or send it critical alerts only.

  4. The app does not encrypt what it keeps for offline use

    To show your servers without a connection, the app keeps what it last showed you, including SSH sign-ins from the timeline, with user names and addresses, and the audit log, with the address each action came from. The app does not encrypt this copy itself. Android keeps it in the app’s own storage, which other apps cannot read, and out of backups. It is deleted when you remove or pair the server again, when the server refuses this phone, or when a guest’s access ends.

    With the app lock on, the app shows it only after your fingerprint or screen lock. The home-screen widget and the Quick Settings tile, if you add them, do not ask: they show from this copy the server’s name, whether all is well or how many alerts are active, and its CPU and memory use (the widget its temperature too). The tile may show on the lock screen as well.

  5. Screenshots are allowed

    While the app is open you can take screenshots, as people who look after servers do. With the app lock on, the recent-apps list shows no picture of the app.

  6. No reliance on obfuscation

    The app relies on no obfuscation and no tamper checks for its security: its core is open source (Pro’s code is not), and it holds no secret of ours.

  7. No outside audit yet

    The last security review, on 2026-10-10, was the project’s own, against OWASP MASVS v2, and left no high or critical finding open. It is not an independent audit.
